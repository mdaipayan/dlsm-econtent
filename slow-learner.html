CO1

Control Points & the Digital Survey Workflow (Simplified)

Think of a digital survey as three simple steps: capture the measurement with an instrument, process it on a computer, and output it as a map. A control point is just a spot on the ground whose exact position is already known and trusted — every other measurement is checked against it.

Control point

A fixed, trusted reference point used to check all other measurements.

Horizontal control

Fixes a point's flat (X, Y) position on the ground.

Vertical control

Fixes a point's height (Z) above a reference level.

Guided worksheet
  1. Look at the workflow diagram above and write, in your own words, what happens in each of the three stages.
  2. Copy the definitions of "control point," "horizontal control" and "vertical control" from your notes, then find one real example of each on your own campus.
  3. Ask a classmate or your teacher to check your answers before moving to the next section.
  4. Attempt three short one-line questions from the solved example sheet given in class.
Tip

Don't try to memorise everything at once. Learn the three-stage workflow first, then add the control-point definitions once that feels solid.

CO2

GPS Made Simple

GPS finds your position by measuring the distance to at least four satellites. Each distance draws an imaginary circle (sphere) around a satellite; where three or more circles cross is your location — this is called trilateration. GPS has three parts, called segments.

Space segment

The satellites orbiting the Earth that broadcast signals.

Control segment

Ground stations that track and correct the satellites.

User segment

Your GPS receiver, which listens to the signals.

Guided worksheet
  1. Look at the trilateration diagram above and label which circle belongs to which satellite.
  2. Match each GPS segment (space / control / user) to its correct one-line description.
  3. In the doubt-clearing session, ask about one GPS error you don't understand (e.g. multipath or ionospheric delay).
  4. Solve two simple positioning numericals using the fully worked example as your template.
CO3

DGPS: Fixing GPS's Small Errors

Standalone GPS is accurate to a few metres — good enough for driving directions, but not for engineering. DGPS uses a second, fixed receiver (the base station) at a known point to work out how much error exists right now, and sends that correction to a moving receiver (the rover), making it far more accurate.

Guided worksheet
  1. Label the base-station and rover on the DGPS diagram above.
  2. Complete the sentence: "Differential correction removes common error by ___."
  3. Solve one guided loop-closure numerical (fully worked), then try a second one on your own.
  4. Compare standalone GPS vs. DGPS using the table from your notes and note two differences.
CO4

Total Station Step-by-Step

A Total Station measures angles and distances in a single sighting and does the maths for you. Before you can use it, you must set it up correctly over the ground point — this is the most important skill to practise first.

Guided worksheet
  1. Label the parts diagram (telescope, EDM unit, keyboard/display, tribrach, prism) on the CO4 page.
  2. Follow this checklist in the lab, ticking off each step with your instructor watching: (1) Centre the tripod over the mark, (2) Level the instrument, (3) Switch on and orient to a known point, (4) Set up the job file.
  3. Repeat the setup once more on your own to build confidence.
  4. Try one guided angle/distance numerical using the fully worked example.
CO5

Mapping Basics

Scale tells you how much smaller the map is than the real ground (e.g. 1 cm on paper = 100 m on the ground). Projection is the method used to flatten the curved Earth onto a flat map. Mapping software falls into three broad categories.

CAD-based

Precise drafting and engineering drawings (e.g. AutoCAD Civil 3D).

GIS

Spatial analysis and thematic maps (e.g. QGIS, ArcGIS).

Field-to-finish

Directly turns instrument readings into map features.

Guided worksheet
  1. Label the globe-to-map projection diagram and scale bar on the CO5 page, then compare it with the scale diagram above.
  2. Solve two guided scale-conversion problems (e.g. "1 cm = 100 m") using the worked example.
  3. Match each software name to its category (CAD-based / GIS / field-to-finish).
  4. In the software-orientation session, import one sample point file into QGIS and describe what you see.
