CO4 Unit IV · Total Station

Total Station Surveying

Course Outcome

CO4: Use total station for land surveying and plotting the details.

Introduction

A Total Station is an electronic/optical surveying instrument that integrates an Electronic Distance Measurement (EDM) device with an electronic theodolite and an onboard microprocessor. In a single sighting it measures horizontal angle, vertical angle and slope distance to a target, then computes and stores coordinates automatically — making it the workhorse instrument of modern ground-based digital surveying.

Instrument Parts

Telescope

Sights the target/prism; carries the electronic angle-measuring sensors.

EDM unit

Emits and receives an infrared or laser signal reflected off a prism to compute distance.

Keyboard & display

Used to input point codes, review readings and control on-board programs.

Tribrach & tripod

Levelling base that centres and stabilises the instrument over the station.

Prism / reflector

Mounted on a target pole at the point being observed, reflects the EDM signal back.

Onboard memory

Stores measured angles, distances and computed coordinates for later download.

Many modern instruments can also measure reflectorless (without a prism) to walls and inaccessible points, over a shorter range.

Setting-Up Procedure

  1. Centring: Position the tripod so the instrument is exactly over the ground station mark, using the optical/laser plummet.
  2. Levelling: Use the tribrach's levelling screws and the circular/electronic bubble to make the vertical axis truly vertical.
  3. Powering on & orientation: Switch on the instrument, set the station coordinates and instrument height, then sight a known backsight point to orient the horizontal circle.
  4. Job/point setup: Create or select a job file, and enter point codes/descriptions for the features to be surveyed.

Measurement of Distance, Angles and Height

Once set up and oriented, every sighting gives three readings: horizontal angle, vertical (zenith) angle and slope distance. Everything else is computed from them.

1. Distance (EDM)

The EDM sends an infrared or laser beam to the prism and measures the returned signal. Phase-shift EDMs compare the phase of the sent and received waves; pulse (time-of-flight) EDMs measure the travel time. Distance = (speed of light × travel time) ÷ 2. Before measuring, set the prism constant and the atmospheric correction (from temperature and pressure, given in ppm), otherwise every distance is wrong by the same amount.

2. Horizontal angle

Set the station coordinates and sight a known backsight point; the instrument sets its horizontal circle to the backsight bearing. Every later reading is then a bearing (whole-circle bearing) from north. Measure important angles on both faces and take the mean.

3. Vertical angle

Total stations read the zenith angle Z (0° straight up, 90° level). Vertical angle above the horizontal = 90° − Z.

4. Height and coordinates

With slope distance SD, zenith angle Z, instrument height HI and target (prism) height HT:

  • Horizontal distance: HD = SD × sin Z
  • Vertical difference: VD = SD × cos Z
  • Reduced level of the point: RLP = RLstation + HI + VD − HT
  • Coordinates: EP = Estation + HD × sin(bearing),   NP = Nstation + HD × cos(bearing)
Worked example

Station: E 1000.000, N 2000.000, RL 300.000 m; HI = 1.500 m. To the prism: SD = 125.000 m, Z = 88°30′00″, bearing = 60°00′00″, HT = 1.800 m.
HD = 125 × sin 88.5° = 124.957 m;   VD = 125 × cos 88.5° = 3.272 m.
RL = 300.000 + 1.500 + 3.272 − 1.800 = 302.972 m.
E = 1000 + 124.957 × sin 60° = 1108.216;   N = 2000 + 124.957 × cos 60° = 2062.479.

Remote height (REM): to find the height of a point where no prism can be held (an overhead wire, the top of a building), first measure to a prism directly below it, then sight the top point. The instrument uses the same horizontal distance and the new zenith angle to compute the height above the prism.

Contouring & Mapping

A contour is a line joining points of equal height; the contour interval is the height difference between two neighbouring contours. Close contours mean a steep slope; widely spaced contours mean gentle ground.

  • Radial (polar) method: from a control station, the total station picks up spot heights all around. Always include breaks of slope, ridges, valleys, drain beds and road edges, not only an even spread of points.
  • Grid method: for flat sites, heights are taken at the corners of a square grid set out on the ground.
  • Processing: points are downloaded to CAD/GIS software (CO5), joined into a TIN (triangulated irregular network) to form a Digital Terrain Model, and contours are interpolated at the chosen interval. Break lines stop the software from drawing contours across a drain or a road edge.

The same field data gives the topographic plan, longitudinal and cross sections, and earthwork volumes.

Errors in Total Station Surveying

Errors are of three kinds by nature: gross errors (blunders such as a wrong prism height or wrong point number; found by checks and removed), systematic errors (same size and sign every time, such as a wrong prism constant or collimation error; removed by calibration, correct settings and face-left/face-right), and random errors (small, unavoidable, both signs; reduced by repeated measurement and treated by statistics). By source they are:

Instrumental

Collimation error, vertical index error, and circle graduation errors from imperfect instrument adjustment.

Personal

Inaccurate centring, levelling, or bisecting of the target by the surveyor.

Natural

Atmospheric refraction, temperature and pressure variation affecting the EDM signal path.

Instrumental errors are largely eliminated by taking observations in both face-left and face-right and averaging the results, along with periodic calibration against manufacturer specifications.

Error Propagation

Every reading has a small random error. When readings are combined in a calculation, their errors combine too. This is error propagation. For independent random errors:

  • Sum or difference of measurements: σtotal = √(σ1² + σ2² + …)
  • Sum of n measurements of equal accuracy σ: σtotal = σ √n
  • An angle error δ (in seconds) at distance D moves the point sideways by D × δ ÷ 206 265

Examples:

  • A traverse has 9 angles, each measured to ±5″. Expected error of their sum = 5 × √9 = ±15″.
  • An angle error of 5″ at 500 m shifts the point by 500 × 5 ÷ 206 265 = 0.012 m (12 mm) sideways. So long sights need more accurate angles.
  • EDM accuracy is quoted as ±(a mm + b ppm). For ±(2 mm + 2 ppm) at 600 m: 2 + 2 × 0.6 = ±3.2 mm.

Survey Specification

A survey specification is the written set of rules a survey must meet, fixed before field work starts, so that the result is fit for its purpose and can be checked. It normally states:

  • The required accuracy (class or order of survey) and the coordinate and height system to use.
  • The instrument class and calibration required (for example angle accuracy in seconds, EDM accuracy in mm + ppm).
  • The observing procedure: number of faces and rounds, maximum sight length, checks to the backsight.
  • Closure limits: allowed angular misclosure (often written as c√n seconds for n stations) and allowed linear misclosure (written as a ratio such as 1 in N).
  • Point density for detail survey, contour interval, and the deliverables: coordinate list, plan, report.

Error propagation is used to choose the instrument and procedure that will meet the specification before going to the field.
