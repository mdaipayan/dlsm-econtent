Mapping Fundamentals & Software

Course Outcome

CO5: Use advanced software for mapping.

Introduction

Once field data has been captured by GPS, DGPS or Total Station, it must be converted into a finished map or spatial database. This final module covers the core mapping concepts and the categories of software used to turn survey coordinates into accurate, well-presented digital maps and Geographic Information System (GIS) layers.

Mapping Fundamentals

Scale

The ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground, determining how much real-world detail can be shown.

Map projection

A mathematical method for representing the curved Earth's surface on a flat map (e.g. UTM, Polyconic), each with its own distortion trade-offs.

Coordinate reference system

The datum and coordinate grid (geographic or projected) that gives every point a unique, standardised location.

Accuracy standards

Positional accuracy requirements (e.g. National Map Accuracy Standards) that a map must meet for its intended use.

Each category serves a different stage of the workflow — field-to-finish software gets raw survey data into digital form quickly, CAD produces precise engineering drawings, and GIS adds analytical and database capability on top of the spatial data.

Working Steps in Automated Mapping

  1. Data import: Coordinate/point data is transferred from the Total Station or GPS controller into the software, usually via a standard exchange format.
  2. Point/feature coding: Field codes recorded during survey (e.g. "BLD" for building corner, "TREE", "MH" for manhole) are used to automatically classify and symbolise points.
  3. Linework generation: Coded points are automatically or manually joined into linework representing boundaries, roads, drainage and other linear features.
  4. Contour interpolation: A Digital Terrain Model (DTM) is built by triangulating elevation points (commonly using a Triangulated Irregular Network, TIN), from which contour lines at a chosen interval are interpolated.
  5. Map layout & symbology: Layers are styled, legends, scale bars, north arrows and grids are added to produce a presentable map sheet.
  6. Export: Final data is exported in formats such as DXF (CAD), Shapefile or GeoTIFF (GIS) for use by other software or teams.

Detailing Process

"Detailing" refers to attaching descriptive attribute data to each mapped feature — for example, a manhole point might carry attributes for depth, material and condition, while a building polygon might carry attributes for use, floors and ownership. In a GIS, these attributes make the map queryable (e.g. "show all manholes deeper than 2 m"), turning a simple drawing into a spatial database. Quality checks — comparing feature counts, verifying topology (no gaps/overlaps), and validating attribute completeness — are the final step before cartographic finishing and delivery.

Choosing the Right Tool

The choice between CAD and GIS software depends on the end use: engineering design and construction layout favour CAD's precision drafting tools, while asset management, spatial analysis and thematic mapping favour GIS's database and analysis capability. Many professional workflows use both together — CAD for the initial engineering plan, GIS for long-term data management.
