CO5 Unit V · Mapping

Mapping Fundamentals & Software

Course Outcome

CO5: Use advanced software for mapping.

Introduction

Once field data has been captured by GPS, DGPS or Total Station, it must be converted into a finished map or spatial database. This final module covers the core mapping concepts and the categories of software used to turn survey coordinates into accurate, well-presented digital maps and Geographic Information System (GIS) layers.

Mapping Fundamentals

Scale

The ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground, determining how much real-world detail can be shown.

Map projection

A mathematical method for representing the curved Earth's surface on a flat map (e.g. UTM, Polyconic), each with its own distortion trade-offs.

Coordinate reference system

The datum and coordinate grid (geographic or projected) that gives every point a unique, standardised location.

Accuracy standards

Positional accuracy requirements (e.g. National Map Accuracy Standards) that a map must meet for its intended use.

Coordinate systems used in India

  • Geographic coordinates: latitude and longitude in degrees, with height. GPS gives them on the WGS84 datum. (1″ of latitude is about 31 m on the ground.)
  • UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator): the world is divided into 60 zones, each 6° of longitude wide. In each zone points have Easting and Northing in metres, with a false easting of 500 000 m at the zone's central meridian. Zone number = whole part of (longitude + 180) ÷ 6, plus 1. India lies in zones 42 to 47; Nagpur and Ramtek (about 79° E) are in zone 44.
  • Older Survey of India maps use the Everest datum with the polyconic projection. The newer Open Series Maps use WGS84 and UTM. Coordinates on the two datums differ by hundreds of metres, so always check the datum before combining old and new data.
  • Heights on maps are reduced levels above mean sea level, carried from benchmarks; GPS heights above the ellipsoid must be converted first.

Height is shown by spot heights (levels written at points), contours (lines of equal height at a fixed contour interval), and in software by a DEM/DTM (a height model), from which contours, sections, slope maps and volumes are produced.

Each category serves a different stage of the workflow — field-to-finish software gets raw survey data into digital form quickly, CAD produces precise engineering drawings, and GIS adds analytical and database capability on top of the spatial data.

Working Steps in Automated Mapping

  1. Data import: Coordinate/point data is transferred from the Total Station or GPS controller into the software, usually via a standard exchange format.
  2. Point/feature coding: Field codes recorded during survey (e.g. "BLD" for building corner, "TREE", "MH" for manhole) are used to automatically classify and symbolise points.
  3. Linework generation: Coded points are automatically or manually joined into linework representing boundaries, roads, drainage and other linear features.
  4. Contour interpolation: A Digital Terrain Model (DTM) is built by triangulating elevation points (commonly using a Triangulated Irregular Network, TIN), from which contour lines at a chosen interval are interpolated.
  5. Map layout & symbology: Layers are styled, legends, scale bars, north arrows and grids are added to produce a presentable map sheet.
  6. Export: Final data is exported in formats such as DXF (CAD), Shapefile or GeoTIFF (GIS) for use by other software or teams.

Establishment of Control Points for Mapping

A digital map is only as accurate as the control it hangs on. Before detail survey starts:

  1. Plan the control so that every part of the area can be reached from at least one control point, with a second point visible for orientation and checks.
  2. Connect to known control: start from a Survey of India benchmark or GNSS control, or use CORS, so the map is on the national coordinate and height system.
  3. Fix the points by GNSS static or RTK survey, or by a closed total-station traverse; adjust the closing error.
  4. Mark and describe each point (pillar or nail, number, description card) so it can be used again for setting out.
  5. For drone mapping, lay out ground control points (GCPs): painted targets spread over the area, including its edges, and fixed by RTK. Keep a few as check points that are not used in processing, to test the final map.

Detailing of Digital Land Surveying

Detailing is the field work of picking up every feature to be shown on the map, measured from the control points, and then building those features in software.

  • Field pick-up: from a control station, the total station (radial method) or an RTK rover records each detail point: building corners, road and kerb edges, boundary walls, poles, trees, manholes, drains, and spot heights.
  • Feature codes: every point gets a code (for example BLD, RD, TREE, MH); line codes tell the software which points to join and in what order.
  • Field sketch: a rough sketch with point numbers helps to sort out any coding mistake in the office.
  • Check shots: re-measure a known point at the start and end of every set-up to prove that nothing moved.
  • In the office: codes build the lines, symbols and layers automatically; then attributes are added (a manhole's depth and material, a building's use and floors), which make the map queryable in a GIS, for example "show all manholes deeper than 2 m".
  • Final checks: missing features, closed building outlines, no gaps or overlaps between parcels (topology), complete attributes, and a comparison with the field sketch.

Choosing the Right Tool

The choice between CAD and GIS software depends on the end use: engineering design and construction layout favour CAD's precision drafting tools, while asset management, spatial analysis and thematic mapping favour GIS's database and analysis capability. Many professional workflows use both together — CAD for the initial engineering plan, GIS for long-term data management.
