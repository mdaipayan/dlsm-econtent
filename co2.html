Global Positioning System (GPS)

Course Outcome

CO2: Handle the GPS for surveying and plot the details on map.

Introduction

The Global Positioning System (GPS) is a satellite-based radio navigation system, operated by the United States, that provides continuous, all-weather, 3D position, velocity and time information anywhere on Earth. It is one of several Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), alongside Russia's GLONASS, the EU's Galileo and China's BeiDou, and is the backbone of modern digital surveying. India has its own regional system, NavIC (IRNSS); modern survey receivers track GPS, NavIC and the other systems together, which gives more satellites and better results near trees and buildings.

Basic idea: each satellite sends its position and the exact time. The receiver measures how long the signal took to arrive, turns that time into a distance (distance = speed of light × travel time), and finds its own position from the distances to several satellites. Three distances would fix a point; a fourth satellite is needed because the receiver's clock is not exact.

GPS Segments

GPS is built from three cooperating segments:

Space segment

A constellation of about 24–32 satellites arranged in 6 orbital planes at roughly 20,200 km altitude, ensuring at least 4 satellites are visible from any point on Earth at any time.

Control segment

A master control station plus a network of monitor stations that track the satellites, compute orbital and clock corrections, and upload them back to the satellites.

User segment

The GPS receivers — handheld, vehicle-mounted, or survey-grade — that pick up satellite signals and compute position, velocity and time.

Signal Structure

Each GPS satellite continuously broadcasts on multiple carrier frequencies (L1 at 1575.42 MHz, L2 at 1227.60 MHz, and the newer L5), modulated with two ranging codes and a navigation message:

  • C/A code (Coarse/Acquisition): Publicly available, broadcast on L1, used by consumer-grade receivers.
  • P(Y) code (Precise): Higher-precision code broadcast on both L1 and L2. It is encrypted (the Y code), so only authorised/military users can use it directly.
  • Modern civil signals: L2C (on L2) and L5 are open to everyone. They let civilian receivers use two frequencies and remove most of the ionospheric delay.
  • Navigation message: Contains satellite orbital parameters (ephemeris), clock correction data, and satellite health status, broadcast at a slow data rate.

GPS User Segment: Types of Receivers

A receiver has an antenna, a radio part that tracks the signals, a processor, memory and a power supply. Survey receivers are grouped in two ways, as the syllabus asks:

  • Code receiver: measures distance (pseudorange) from the ranging code. Simple, low cost, gives a position immediately; used in phones, navigation units and handheld GIS receivers.
  • Carrier-phase (frequency) receiver: also measures the phase of the carrier wave itself, whose wavelength is only about 19 cm on L1. Used with a second receiver or a correction service, this gives centimetre-level results; used for control and engineering survey.
  • Single-frequency receiver: uses L1 only; cannot remove ionospheric delay, so it is used for short baselines.
  • Dual/multi-frequency receiver: uses L1 with L2 and/or L5; removes most of the ionospheric delay, so it works over long baselines and fixes its solution faster.

Survey-grade GPS/GNSS receivers use carrier-phase measurements together with differential or RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) correction techniques to achieve centimetre-level accuracy — the same principle explored in the DGPS module.

Positioning Methods & Field/Office Software

  • Absolute (point) positioning: A single receiver computes its position independently from at least 4 satellites — simplest but least accurate method.
  • Relative (differential) positioning: Two or more receivers observe the same satellites simultaneously; comparing their data cancels out common errors, discussed further under DGPS.

RINEX (Receiver Independent Exchange format) is the standard text format for raw GNSS data, so files from any receiver brand can be processed together.

Steps of a GPS survey

  1. Planning: decide accuracy and method; check satellite availability and DOP for the day (mission planning); choose control points.
  2. Reconnaissance: visit the site; choose points with open sky and no reflecting surfaces nearby.
  3. Field observation: set up over the point, centre and level the antenna, measure and record the antenna height, log data for the planned time, record point codes and a field sketch.
  4. Download and convert: transfer raw files to the computer; convert to RINEX if needed.
  5. Processing: compute baselines between receivers, check loop closures, adjust the network holding known control fixed (detailed in CO3).
  6. Quality check and report: look at the accuracy of every point; re-observe weak points.
  7. Plot: transform to the map coordinate system and plot the details (next section).

Plotting GPS Details on a Map

CO2 asks students to plot the surveyed details on a map. The steps:

  1. Choose the coordinate system. GPS gives latitude, longitude and ellipsoidal height on the WGS84 datum. For an engineering map use projected coordinates in metres, usually UTM (Easting, Northing). India lies in UTM zones 42 to 47; Nagpur and Ramtek are in zone 44 (78° E to 84° E).
  2. Export the points as a CSV file with columns: point number, Easting, Northing, height, code.
  3. Import into QGIS (Layer › Add Delimited Text Layer) or a CAD program, setting the same coordinate system, for example WGS 84 / UTM zone 44N.
  4. Join and symbolise: join points of the same code into lines and polygons (road edges, building outlines); give symbols to trees, poles and manholes.
  5. Finish the map: add a grid, north arrow, scale bar, legend and title (CO5 covers this in detail).
Height warning

GPS height is measured above the WGS84 ellipsoid, not above mean sea level. The two differ by the geoid height, which in India can be tens of metres. For levels tied to benchmarks, use a geoid model or connect to a benchmark.

Errors in GPS Observations

Satellite clock

Tiny timing offsets in the satellite's atomic clock translate directly into ranging error.

Ephemeris error

Small inaccuracies in the broadcast orbital data used to compute satellite position.

Ionospheric delay

Charged particles in the ionosphere slow the signal, the single largest error source for single-frequency receivers.

Tropospheric delay

Water vapour in the lower atmosphere also delays the signal, though less than the ionosphere.

Multipath

Signal reflecting off buildings, water or terrain before reaching the receiver, causing position jitter.

Dilution of precision (DOP)

Poor satellite geometry (satellites clustered together) amplifies the effect of all other errors.

Receiver clock & noise

The receiver's cheap quartz clock is not exact (solved by using a fourth satellite), and electronic noise adds small random errors.

Set-up errors

Wrong centring, a mistake in the measured antenna height, or obstructions near the antenna; these are human errors that no processing can remove.

Key idea

Most of these errors are common to any two nearby receivers observing the same satellites at the same time — which is exactly what Differential GPS exploits to cancel them out (see CO3).
