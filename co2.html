Global Positioning System (GPS)

Course Outcome

CO2: Handle the GPS for surveying and plot the details on map.

Introduction

The Global Positioning System (GPS) is a satellite-based radio navigation system, operated by the United States, that provides continuous, all-weather, 3D position, velocity and time information anywhere on Earth. It is one of several Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), alongside Russia's GLONASS, the EU's Galileo and China's BeiDou, and is the backbone of modern digital surveying.

GPS Segments

GPS is built from three cooperating segments:

Space segment

A constellation of about 24–32 satellites arranged in 6 orbital planes at roughly 20,200 km altitude, ensuring at least 4 satellites are visible from any point on Earth at any time.

Control segment

A master control station plus a network of monitor stations that track the satellites, compute orbital and clock corrections, and upload them back to the satellites.

User segment

The GPS receivers — handheld, vehicle-mounted, or survey-grade — that pick up satellite signals and compute position, velocity and time.

Signal Structure

Each GPS satellite continuously broadcasts on multiple carrier frequencies (L1 at 1575.42 MHz, L2 at 1227.60 MHz, and the newer L5), modulated with two ranging codes and a navigation message:

  • C/A code (Coarse/Acquisition): Publicly available, broadcast on L1, used by consumer-grade receivers.
  • P(Y) code (Precise): Higher-precision code broadcast on both L1 and L2, primarily for authorised/military use.
  • Navigation message: Contains satellite orbital parameters (ephemeris), clock correction data, and satellite health status, broadcast at a slow data rate.

Types of Receivers

Carrier-phase (survey-grade)Carrier wave phase measurementSub-centimetre to few cm (with correction)

Survey-grade GPS/GNSS receivers use carrier-phase measurements together with differential or RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) correction techniques to achieve centimetre-level accuracy — the same principle explored in the DGPS module.

Positioning Methods & Field/Office Software

  • Absolute (point) positioning: A single receiver computes its position independently from at least 4 satellites — simplest but least accurate method.
  • Relative (differential) positioning: Two or more receivers observe the same satellites simultaneously; comparing their data cancels out common errors, discussed further under DGPS.

In the field, data-logging software on the receiver/controller records raw observations and point codes. In the office, baseline-processing software computes precise coordinates from the raw data, applies corrections, and exports results to CAD/GIS mapping packages for plotting.

Sources of GPS Error

Satellite clock

Tiny timing offsets in the satellite's atomic clock translate directly into ranging error.

Ephemeris error

Small inaccuracies in the broadcast orbital data used to compute satellite position.

Ionospheric delay

Charged particles in the ionosphere slow the signal, the single largest error source for single-frequency receivers.

Tropospheric delay

Water vapour in the lower atmosphere also delays the signal, though less than the ionosphere.

Multipath

Signal reflecting off buildings, water or terrain before reaching the receiver, causing position jitter.

Dilution of precision (DOP)

Poor satellite geometry (satellites clustered together) amplifies the effect of all other errors.

Key idea

Most of these errors are common to any two nearby receivers observing the same satellites at the same time — which is exactly what Differential GPS exploits to cancel them out (see CO3).
